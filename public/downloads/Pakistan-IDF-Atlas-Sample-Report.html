2.0 Purpose and how to read this report

This report estimates rainfall intensity for selected storm durations and annual exceedance probabilities from the active dataset. It documents the data used, the checks applied, the fitted method, the uncertainty and the limits on engineering use. It is a technical analysis record, not regulatory approval or independent design certification.

What was analysed?

The uploaded daily gauge series for SYNTHETIC DEMONSTRATION — not measured rainfall. District boundaries are not used in the calculation.

What does “T-year” mean?

A T-year value has a 1/T chance of being exceeded in any year. For example, a 100-year value has 1% annual exceedance probability; it does not occur on a fixed 100-year schedule.

Which durations are measured?

Daily data directly support 24 hours only. All shorter durations are derived with the stated scaling equation.

How should rare values be used?

Results for 250 and 500 years are model extrapolations well beyond most records. Use them only with uncertainty, method sensitivity and independent hydrologic review.

3.2 Quality-control rules

Dates are sorted and de-duplicated, non-numeric or negative precipitation values are rejected, missing calendar days are counted, and only years with at least 90% expected daily coverage are eligible for the annual-maxima series. These automated checks support traceability but do not replace metadata-led homogenization of station relocations, instruments or observation practices, consistent with WMO climate-data guidance.

4.0 Methodology

The workflow follows an annual-maxima frequency-analysis structure commonly used in hydrology (Hosking, 1990; Katz et al., 2002). Equation definitions are reproduced in Appendix A.

From input to reported intensity: step by step

  1. Identify and read the source. Use SYNTHETIC DEMONSTRATION — not measured rainfall · observed daily. Confirm location, native interval, units, timestamps, date range and actual retrieval/import success.
  2. Clean records. Sort dates, remove/reconcile duplicates and reject invalid or negative precipitation according to QC. Count missing intervals; distinguish missing observations from measured zero rainfall.
  3. Retain eligible years. Compare accepted and expected intervals for each year. Keep years meeting the 90% coverage rule and document excluded years. Remaining gaps can still miss extremes.
  4. Extract annual maxima. Retain the largest eligible 24-hour depth in each year. Sub-daily values derived later are not observed sub-daily annual maxima.
  5. Fit the selected frequency model. Use the eligible annual-maxima sample to estimate the chosen distribution parameters, or match covariates and fit a nonstationary GEV. Check model status, sample length and diagnostics.
  6. Calculate return levels. For each selected T, evaluate the fitted distribution at F=1−1/T to obtain rainfall depth. State the reference year or covariate for a nonstationary model.
  7. Build the duration curves. Keep the fitted 24-hour return level as the base. Apply only the stated duration-scaling or observed-ratio method for shorter durations; a 24-hour nonstationary fit does not independently fit a nonstationary model for each hourly duration.
  8. Convert depth to intensity. Use i=60P/t with minutes and millimetres. Check that the 24-hour row satisfies P=24i.
  9. Fit chart equations and calculate a requested duration. Regress ln(i) on ln(t) for each T to obtain c, p and log-space R². Evaluate i=c×tᵖ only within the fitted duration range. This final regression summarizes the frequency-model outputs.
  10. Review and preserve the evidence. Inspect fit diagnostics, trend, uncertainty and method sensitivity. Export the report with the Excel workbook, source metadata and source file, then obtain independent review for design use.

For retrieved CMIP6 series, the climate workflow additionally verifies units/calendar and applies the selected historical-reference correction before fitting future extremes. Read the active correction status; this sequence does not imply that correction has already been completed.

4.3 Stationary, nonstationary and reference-year interpretation

Stationary means the probability distribution used for annual precipitation maxima has parameters that do not change with time or another predictor. A 100-year intensity therefore has the same model value in every year. Nonstationary means at least one parameter or return level changes with a covariate such as time, temperature, ENSO or urbanization. The return level is conditional on a stated covariate value, so it must be reported with that value rather than as a timeless quantity.

Reference-year rule. The software default is calculated automatically as ȳ=Σyᵢ/n using only eligible annual-maxima years. Centering is a regression re-parameterization: it makes μ₀ the fitted location at the centre of the observed time covariate without changing μ₁ or the fitted trend. Cheng and AghaKouchak (2014) and Sarhadi and Soulis (2017) formulate GEV parameters as functions of time; Agilan and Umamahesh (2017, 2018) emphasize covariate choice and uncertainty. Literature does not prescribe one universal future reference year. A latest or custom year is therefore an evaluation choice, and a year outside the eligible AMS span is explicitly classified as extrapolation rather than a climate projection.

4.3 Calculation sequence and model definitions

  1. Ingestion and QC. Parse the selected native timestamp and precipitation columns; apply the custom date range; retain one record per day or hour; reject non-numeric or negative precipitation; and inventory missing, duplicate and invalid records.
  2. Annual-maxima series. Retain each calendar year with at least 90% coverage at the selected native interval. Daily input yields one-day maxima; hourly input yields rolling maxima for each supported 1- to 24-hour duration. The software requires at least 8 eligible maxima for fitting and at least 30 before issuing a diagnostic preference based on the lowest RMSE.
  3. Stationarity assessment. Apply the two-sided Mann–Kendall monotonic-trend test at α=0.05 and estimate Sen’s median slope (Mann, 1945). Lag-1 correlation is reported as an independence diagnostic; the p-value is not adjusted for serial correlation.
  4. Stationary probability distributions. Fit six alternatives to the same annual-maxima sample: GEV by L-moments; Gumbel EV1 by sample moments; two-parameter Lognormal in natural-log space; two-parameter Gamma from the sample mean and variance; two-parameter Weibull from the sample coefficient of variation; and Log-Pearson III in base-10 log space. LP3 is an implemented comparison, not a claim of full Bulletin 17C compliance. Quantiles are compared at Gringorten plotting positions using RMSE on the 24-hour depth scale. Sherman is excluded from this distribution ranking because it is an IDF equation, not a probability model.
  5. Sherman representation. Regress GEV return levels into a constrained power law in return period and duration with b=0. The Sherman curve is therefore a compact representation of the GEV-based IDF surface, not an independent probability distribution.
  6. Duration and intensity conversion. For daily input, preserve 24 hours as the observation-supported duration and derive shorter durations with the selected power exponent. For hourly input, fit each rolling 1-, 2-, 3-, 6-, 12- and 24-hour annual-maxima series separately; derive only the 5-, 10-, 15- and 30-minute depths from the 1-hour fit. Divide depth by duration in hours to obtain mm/hour.
  7. Uncertainty. At every fitted duration, resample the eligible annual-maxima series 200 times with replacement and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. These intervals describe sampling variability conditional on the selected distribution and duration-support rule.
  8. Reference-year calculation. Calculate ȳ=Σyᵢ/n from the years that survive annual completeness screening. Use ȳ by default, the last eligible yᵢ for the latest-supported option, or a declared custom year. Classify custom values outside min(yᵢ)–max(yᵢ) as extrapolation.
  9. Time-varying GEV. Fit stationary and nonstationary GEV models by maximum likelihood to the same annual maxima. The nonstationary model uses μ(y)=μ₀+μ₁(y−ȳ) with constant scale and shape; compare models using AICc and report conditional return levels at the calculated or selected reference year with paired-bootstrap intervals.
  10. Covariate comparison. Match annual ENSO, temperature, moisture, urbanization or another physically justified series to the same eligible annual maxima. Fit a GEV location coefficient for each candidate, compare AICc on the common years, examine probability-integral-transform residuals, quantify the coefficient with a 95% moving-block bootstrap, and evaluate the chronologically held-out final 25% of years using mean negative log-likelihood. Correlation is not used to rank or select a model.

4.4 Interpretation rule

Interpretation boundary. Only the 24-hour values are directly supported by the uploaded daily series. Durations below 24 hours use P(t)=P(24h)(t/24h)^0.33 and are derived estimates, not observed sub-daily extremes.

6.0 Uncertainty, limitations and robustness

  • Daily input directly supports only the 24-hour frequency estimate. Engineering-grade sub-daily IDF requires duration-specific observed maxima.
  • Automated QC does not homogenize station moves, gauge changes, observation-time changes or undocumented corrections; station metadata and neighbouring-gauge comparison remain necessary.
  • The Mann–Kendall test is unadjusted for serial dependence and has limited power in short records. Failure to reject the no-trend null is not proof of stationarity.
  • Bootstrap limits quantify sampling variability only. They exclude structural model choice, duration scaling, gauge undercatch, spatial aggregation and boundary uncertainty.
  • Return periods substantially longer than the eligible record are extrapolations and require sensitivity testing across methods and influential events.
  • District-scale interpretation requires a documented point-to-polygon or grid-area aggregation rule and a verified administrative-boundary vintage.

Report status: Suitable for hydrologic review with the stated limitations; not independently certified for design adoption.

7.0 Conclusions and recommendations

7.1 Conclusion

Stationarity is not rejected and 35 eligible maxima are available, but this is not proof of stationarity and rare return levels remain model-dependent.

7.2 Recommendations

  1. Confirm station or product metadata, precipitation units, daily accumulation time and missing-value codes before accepting the record for design.
  2. Use observed 5-minute, hourly or other duration-specific annual maxima for sub-daily IDF. Where unavailable, label only the 24-hour result as directly supported by the daily source.
  3. Review probability plots, residuals, extreme-event influence and bootstrap stability across all six fitted distributions; do not choose a method from RMSE alone.
  4. Preserve the online provider, product, retrieval time, requested geometry, grid details and returned period with every result; assess satellite or reanalysis bias against quality-controlled local gauges where available.
  5. For time nonstationarity, center time near the record midpoint, report whether the evaluation year is interpolation or extrapolation, and compare the fitted model against the stationary alternative.
  6. For ENSO, match an annual or seasonally justified Niño 3.4/ONI series to each annual maximum, estimate the coefficient rather than assuming it, evaluate lag and season definitions, and verify spatially varying relationships for the selected Pakistan district.
  7. Compare time, ENSO and other physically plausible covariates using AICc, residual diagnostics, parameter uncertainty and out-of-sample performance; do not select a covariate from correlation alone.
  8. Obtain independent hydrologic and engineering review before applying any return level to drainage, flood-control or infrastructure design.

Appendix A · Equations and parameter definitions

Equations below explain the implemented methods. Only the selected source and model contribute to the active results. Symbols are local to each equation: for example, k may mean distribution shape or parameter count. Numerical constants come from unit definitions, published empirical formulas, fixed software rules or the active fitted data, as stated below.

A.2 Equation-by-equation explanation

A1 · Return period and exceedance

AEP = 1/T; F = 1 − 1/T

T is the selected return period in years; AEP is a probability, and F is the non-exceedance probability supplied to the fitted distribution. Multiply AEP by 100 for percent. T is a design/user selection, not estimated from the number of observations. For T=100, F=0.99 and AEP=1%. In nonstationary analysis these probabilities refer to the stated year or covariate value.

A2 · Coverage and annual maxima

coverage = valid intervals / expected intervals; Mᵧ,ᴅ = max(eligible duration-D totals in year y)

Valid intervals come from QC-accepted dates/timestamps. Expected counts follow the calendar and native interval. The 0.90 coverage threshold is a fixed software eligibility rule, not a fitted constant. D is the duration: daily data supply one-day totals; hourly windows must contain consecutive observations. n counts retained years, not daily records. The minimum 8 stationary or 20 nonstationary maxima is an eligibility threshold, not proof of accuracy.

A3 · Rainfall depth and intensity

i = 60P/t; P = it/60

P is accumulated depth in mm, t is duration in minutes and i is mean intensity in mm/hour. The number 60 converts minutes to hours. For 24 hours, t=1440 and i=P/24. Depth comes from a fitted return level or an explicitly derived duration; intensity is a unit conversion, not another independent measurement.

A4 · Daily duration scaling

P(t) = P₂₄(t/1440)^α; i(t) = (P₂₄/24)(t/1440)^(α−1)

P₂₄ is the selected 24-hour fitted depth in mm; t is minutes. α is the user-selected duration-scaling exponent, not a value fitted from a daily record. With α=0.33, the intensity exponent is −0.67. Constants 1440 and 24 are minutes and hours per day. This rule produces derived sub-daily values. Native hourly fits and observed-ratio CMIP6 workflows use their documented duration treatment instead; a pure scaling exponent must not be described as measured sub-daily evidence.

A5 · Power trendline for one return period

i = c × tᵖ; X = ln(t); Y = ln(i); p = Σ[(X−X̄)(Y−Ȳ)] / Σ[(X−X̄)²]; c = exp(Ȳ−pX̄)

One log–log least-squares regression is fitted to the positive displayed duration/intensity pairs for each T separately. X̄ and Ȳ are means of log durations and log intensities. c is the fitted intercept transformed back to intensity units; p is the fitted duration slope and is dimensionless. c depends on using minutes and mm/hour. The implied value at one minute is outside the usual fitted range and is not an observed one-minute intensity. Neither c nor p is copied from the Excel screenshot.

A6 · Trendline goodness of fit

R² = 1 − Σ(Y−Ŷ)² / Σ(Y−Ȳ)²; Ŷ = ln(c) + p ln(t)

Y is log intensity and Ŷ is its fitted log value. The numerator is residual sum of squares and the denominator is total log-space variation. The implementation bounds displayed R² to [0,1]. R² near one shows that a power equation reproduces the plotted points; it does not validate the rainfall data or extreme-value model. An exact power disaggregation can yield R²=1 by construction.

A7 · GEV return level

z_T = μ + (σ/ξ)[{−ln(F)}^(−ξ) − 1]

z_T is depth in mm, μ is location in mm, σ>0 is scale in mm, ξ is dimensionless tail shape, and F=1−1/T. Stationary GEV uses sample L-moments of eligible annual maxima. Sorted observations supply probability-weighted moments b₀, b₁ and b₂ using rank weights 1, j/(N−1) and j(j−1)/[(N−1)(N−2)] for zero-based ascending rank j, averaged across N positive maxima. Then L₂=2b₁−b₀, L₃=6b₂−6b₁+b₀ and τ₃=L₃/L₂. Set C=2/(3+τ₃)−ln(2)/ln(3); the implemented shape approximation is k=7.859C+2.9554C². The numbers 7.859 and 2.9554 are fixed L-moment approximation coefficients, not locally fitted rainfall constants. Scale follows σ=L₂k/[(1−2^(−k))Γ(1+k)] and location μ=b₀−σ[1−Γ(1+k)]/k, with a separate near-zero-k limit. The stationary implementation uses the opposite shape sign k=−ξ internally; this is a parameter convention, not a different distribution. As ξ→0, use the Gumbel limit. Nonstationary GEV parameters are estimated by MLE, not L-moments. Actual values must be read from the active fit/diagnostic output, not assumed from this generic formula.

A8 · Gumbel EV1

z_T = μ − β ln[−ln(F)]; β = s√6/π; μ = x̄ − γβ

x̄ and s are the mean and sample standard deviation of the positive annual-maxima depths. β is scale in mm and μ is location in mm, estimated by sample moments. π is the mathematical constant 3.14159… and γ≈0.5772156649 is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. These two constants are not calibrated to the rainfall record.

A9 · Log-Pearson III

log₁₀(z_T) = μ_log + K(T,G)σ_log

Take base-10 logs of positive annual maxima. μ_log and σ_log are their mean and sample standard deviation; G is adjusted sample skew in log space. K is the Pearson III frequency factor determined by T and G through the distribution quantile, not a fixed user constant. The implementation evaluates a shifted/scaled Gamma quantile with skew sign handling; the near-zero-skew limit is normal in log space. This comparison is not a full Bulletin 17C analysis.

A10 · Lognormal

z_T = exp[μ_ln + σ_ln Φ⁻¹(F)]

μ_ln and σ_ln are the mean and sample standard deviation of natural-log positive annual maxima. Φ⁻¹ is the standard-normal quantile at F, computed mathematically, not a rainfall fitting constant. exp converts back to mm. Natural logs here must not be mixed with the base-10 logs used for LP3.

A11 · Gamma

z_T = θ G⁻¹(F;k); k = (x̄/s)²; θ = s²/x̄

x̄ and s come from positive annual-maxima depths. k is dimensionless shape; θ is scale in mm. G⁻¹ is the unit-scale Gamma quantile, numerically inverted at F. These are moment estimates from the sample. G in this expression means a Gamma distribution function, whereas G in LP3 denotes log-space skew.

A12 · Weibull

z_T = λ[ln(T)]^(1/k); Γ(1+2/k)/Γ(1+1/k)² = 1+(s/x̄)²; λ = x̄/Γ(1+1/k)

k is dimensionless shape, solved numerically from the sample coefficient of variation s/x̄; λ is scale in mm. Γ is the mathematical Gamma function, not the Gamma rainfall distribution. Both fitted parameters depend on eligible positive annual maxima. The equation follows from F=1−1/T; T remains the selected return period.

A13 · Global Sherman representation

i(t,T) = aTᵐ/(t+b)ⁿ; b=0; ln(i)=ln(a)+m ln(T)−n ln(t)

a, m and n are jointly fitted by log-space least squares across positive active duration/return-period rows. a is a unit-dependent scale; m controls change with return period; n controls decline with duration. b is fixed at zero in this implementation, not fitted. Use minutes and mm/hour. This equation summarizes the IDF surface and may differ from a separate power fit for one T. It is a representation of fitted values, not an independent probability distribution.

A14 · Nonstationary location and model comparison

μ(y)=μ₀+μ₁(y−ȳ); μ(x)=μ₀+β₁[(x−x̄)/sₓ]; AICc=2k−2ln(L)+2k(k+1)/(n−k−1)

y is calendar year and ȳ is the mean eligible year. x is an annual matched covariate; x̄ and sₓ are its sample mean and standard deviation. MLE estimates the intercept, slope, scale and shape; σ and ξ are constant while location varies. A standardized covariate slope β₁ is converted to physical units as β₁/sₓ. AICc uses maximum likelihood L, parameter count k and common sample size n; smaller values are preferred and |ΔAICc|≤2 is treated as inconclusive. Symbols k and n here count parameters/observations, not Weibull shape or Sherman exponent.

A15 · Trend and diagnostic statistics

S=Σᵢ<ⱼ sign(Mⱼ−Mᵢ); Sen slope=median[(Mⱼ−Mᵢ)/(yⱼ−yᵢ)]; RMSE=√[Σ(z_fit−z_empirical)²/N]

M is annual-maxima depth. Mann–Kendall compares all year pairs, applies tie-corrected variance and reports a two-sided p-value against α=0.05; this fixed test threshold differs from the duration exponent α. Sen slope is in mm/year. The p-value is not adjusted for serial correlation. RMSE compares fitted and empirical quantiles using Gringorten non-exceedance plotting positions (r−0.44)/(N+0.12) for ascending rank r. N counts compared points, and 0.44/0.12 are fixed plotting-position constants.

A16 · Kirpich travel time and flow-path slope

t_c = 0.0195 L^0.77 S^(−0.385); S=(z_up−z_out)/L; S₁₀–₈₅=(z₈₅−z₁₀)/(0.75L)

L is flow-path length in metres; S is slope in m/m; t_c is minutes. Convert a percent slope to m/m by dividing by 100. The coefficient 0.0195 and exponents 0.77 and −0.385 are fixed empirical metric Kirpich constants, not fitted from rainfall. Geometry is entered manually or derived from the selected path and elevation source. z₁₀ and z₈₅ are elevations at 10% and 85% distance from the outlet; 0.75 is their fractional separation. Verify catchment applicability before adoption; rainfall depth at t_c is i(t_c)t_c/60.

A18 · Bootstrap intervals

For a 95% interval, the workflow repeatedly resamples eligible annual maxima, refits the selected model and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting estimates. Nonstationary coefficient intervals use the documented moving-block bootstrap. The 95%, 2.5% and 97.5% values define the reporting interval; they are not rainfall constants. Resampling describes uncertainty conditional on the sample and model, and excludes unmodelled provider, scenario and structural errors.
